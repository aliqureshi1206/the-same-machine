01 — PREMISE

Memory is not a recording. It is a construction kit.

For three decades the Schacter Memory Lab at Harvard has argued something that sounds wrong at first. The main job of episodic memory may not be to preserve the past. Memory is constructive. Each recollection is rebuilt from scattered fragments, and the system that rebuilds it is the same one we use to imagine things that have not happened yet.

This is the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis1. It explains two things together. We can picture tomorrow's job interview because we can flexibly recombine pieces of past experience. The same flexibility is why memory goes wrong: details migrate, gaps get filled, and a retold story slowly becomes a different story2.

If remembering and imagining really run on the same machine, the stories people produce in each mode should look alike. They should not look identical. The hypothesis predicts specific places where they differ, and the reality-monitoring literature3 says where to look: perceived events leave sensory and contextual traces, while imagined ones carry more traces of cognitive operations, the effort of making things up.

This project tests those predictions at a scale lab studies rarely reach. It uses every story in the Hippocorpus and scores each word against three large sets of human-rated norms.

03 — THE SIGNATURE

Memories are anchored. Imaginings think out loud.

Each of the 6,854 stories was scored on 25 measures. Some come from published norms: how concrete each word is (37,058 rated words), how strongly it evokes each of six senses (39,707 words), and how pleasant and arousing it is (13,905 words). Others are counts that track the Autobiographical Interview's split between internal (episodic) and external (semantic) detail4: clock and calendar anchors, numerals, proper nouns, thinking verbs, hedges and generalities.

The chart compares recalled and imagined stories on every measure. Points to the right mean the measure is higher in real memories.

The visual sense separates memory from imagination best. Imagined stories lean toward the body: taste, and especially interoception, the felt sense of one's own insides.

04 — THE TERRITORY

Six thousand lives, one landscape

Each story was projected into a semantic space (TF-IDF → 80-dimension SVD → t-SNE), so stories about similar events sit near each other. Scroll to explore the map. Hover over any dot to see what the story is about.

Every dot is a story

Altogether 6,854 of them, from 3,640 people. The territory looks the way human life does: weddings, funerals, births, road trips, job offers, hospital rooms.

Now colour them by origin

Remembered, imagined and retold stories are thoroughly intermixed. Imagination doesn't use a separate vocabulary of events. It works in exactly the same semantic territory as memory, as the shared-system view predicts.

Sixteen neighbourhoods of life

K-means on the semantic space finds sixteen clusters of events. Imagined stories are over-represented in just two: conversations and the abstract "feelings and life" cluster. When concrete detail runs out, invented events drift toward dialogue and reflection.

How memory-like does each story read?

Colour now shows a cross-validated model's estimate that a story is a real memory, using the 25 language measures. The gradient runs through every neighbourhood: some imagined stories read as utterly real, and some real memories read as invented.

Where invention shows most

Each label now shows the topic's gap in concreteness between real and imagined versions. It is largest for car accidents, hospitals and the outdoors, physical, spatially complex events that are hard to fake, and smallest for conversations, pets and reflection.
05 — DISTORTION

The memories we revisit most change the most

Months after writing their memory, 1,310 people told the same event again. These pairs let us watch memory reconstruct itself in natural language: same person, same event, different moment of retrieval.

This is the counter-intuitive result. You might expect the memories we rehearse most to be the most stable. Instead, frequently revisited and emotionally significant memories change more between tellings. That fits a reconstructive account in which every retrieval is a new construction that can update the trace, and it echoes the finding that vivid, important "flashbulb" memories feel certain while being no more consistent than ordinary ones5.

06 — AGE

Whatever age does to the past, it does to the future

A key piece of evidence for the shared-system view is that ageing changes remembered and imagined events in parallel. Older adults produce fewer internal (episodic) details and more external (semantic) ones both when remembering past events and when imagining future ones6. The Hippocorpus authors span 18 to 55+, which is a narrower window than lab ageing studies, but it lets us test the parallel-slopes prediction directly.

08 — WHERE THIS COULD GO

Four studies this points toward

This corpus is observational and was not collected under lab control, so every result here is a hypothesis generator, not a test. Each one maps onto an active line of work in the lab.

Aging & memory

Extend the parallel-slopes test past 65

Collect Hippocorpus-style recalled/imagined pairs from adults aged 18–85, scored with both the Autobiographical Interview and the automated norm-based measures above. Test whether the automated measures recover the known internal-detail decline, and whether past and future slopes stay parallel in late life.

Can norm-based scoring replace hours of manual AI coding at scale?
Constructive memory & distortion

Rehearsal as a driver of drift

Why do frequently rehearsed memories change more? Experimentally manipulate retrieval frequency and retrieval mode (specific vs. general) between two tellings, and measure drift with the same similarity metric, plus whether imagined details enter the retelling.

Does sensory-rich initial encoding protect against reconstruction?
Episodic simulation

A language-level reality-monitoring index

The perceptual-versus-reflective axis found here could become a continuous, automated index of "episodicness" for any narrative. Validate it against fMRI activity in the core network during simulation, and against later source-memory confusions.

Do imagined events that score as memory-like get misremembered as real?
Creativity & problem solving

Specificity induction, measured in language

The episodic specificity induction boosts divergent thinking7. Does it also move a person's imagined narratives along the perceptual axis? Openness barely predicts narrative style here (|ρ| < .07), which suggests style is a state that can be induced, not a trait.

09 — METHODS & LIMITATIONS

How this was built

Data. Hippocorpus v3 (Sap, Horvitz, Choi, Smith & Pennebaker, 2020; Microsoft Research): 6,854 stories, 1.8 M words, 120,464 sentences, 3,640 crowdworkers. Lexical norms: Brysbaert, Warriner & Kuperman (2014) concreteness; Lynott et al. (2020) Lancaster Sensorimotor Norms; Warriner, Kuperman & Brysbaert (2013) valence/arousal. Nothing on this page is simulated; every number is computed from these sources.

Pipeline. One reproducible Python script (analysis/build.py) tokenises each story, looks up norms for content words (stop words removed), computes the 25 measures, and runs every analysis. Effect sizes come with bootstrap or analytic 95% CIs. The classifier is logistic regression with grouped 5-fold CV by author. Similarity is TF-IDF cosine.

MeasureDefinition

Limitations, stated plainly.

  • Imagined stories were written from a summary, so this measures "imagining someone else's event as your own", not simulating one's own future. It is a close cousin of episodic simulation but not the same paradigm.
  • Word-norm averages are proxies for internal and external detail. They are not Autobiographical Interview scores and have not been validated against them here.
  • Authors are online crowdworkers aged 18–55+, and story length and effort differ by condition. Effect sizes are small (|d| ≤ 0.42) and should be read as such.
  • The "time since event" variable is restricted by design (recalled events were from roughly the last six months), so forgetting curves cannot be estimated properly.
  • Some prompt wording leaks into the text (e.g. "memorable event"), which slightly inflates text-based classification.
